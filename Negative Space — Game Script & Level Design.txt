Negative Space — Game Script & Level Design
Proof of Concept — 8 Levels
A note on structure: each level introduces one new mechanical idea and one new emotional beat. The mechanics escalate. The shadow's voice shifts across the arc — distant and bored at first, then gradually more present as the final human approaches. Dialogue is sparse by design. Silence is the shadow's natural language.

Level 1 — The Storage Room
Setting: A back room of a 24-hour convenience store. Fluorescent lights hum behind a closed door. Shelves cast deep shadows. One flickering bulb overhead.
Mechanic introduced: Basic shadow movement. The player can only travel through connected dark areas. Light kills instantly.
Design: Pure tutorial. The flickering bulb teaches timing — every few seconds the room goes dark and the shadow can cross open ground. No enemies. No dialogue from anyone but the shadow.
Shadow's internal monologue (sparse, on-screen text):

Another room. Another century. The light here is cheap and tired. It will not last the night.

Why it doesn't get old: It's the shortest level. Two minutes. The player learns the rule and the tone simultaneously, then it ends before the novelty fades.

Level 2 — The Parking Garage
Setting: Underground parking. Pillars cast long shadows under sodium lamps. A security guard patrols with a flashlight.
Mechanic introduced: Moving light sources. The shadow must track the guard's flashlight cone and reposition continuously.
Design: The guard walks a fixed loop. The shadow learns to read the anticipation of light, not just react to it. One scripted moment: the guard stops, looks directly at the pillar the shadow is hiding behind, then moves on. Nothing happens. But the player feels seen.
Overheard dialogue (guard, on phone):

"Yeah I know, I know. Double shift again. Beth's pissed. I told her, the lights stay on, somebody's gotta watch them."

Shadow's monologue (after the guard passes):

He guards the light. The light does not guard him.

Why it doesn't get old: The patrol pattern is short, but the level introduces tension without failure. The player escapes something that was never actually hunting them.

Level 3 — The Apartment
Setting: A small studio apartment at night. A woman is awake, lit only by her laptop screen. The shadow moves through her furniture, behind her, around her.
Mechanic introduced: Soft light. Some light sources don't kill — they weaken. The shadow moves slower through dim ambient glow, faster through deep dark.
Design: No enemies. No fail state. The level is about presence. The woman occasionally gets up — to refill water, to check her phone — and the shadow has to move out of her way without her noticing. She never does.
Overheard, the woman talking to herself:

"Okay. Okay. I'll send it tomorrow. Tomorrow morning. First thing."

She closes the laptop. The room goes nearly black. The shadow expands across the entire floor.
Shadow's monologue:

She thinks she is alone. She has never been alone.

Why it doesn't get old: It's the first level with a human you don't have to escape. It reframes the shadow's relationship to people. Not predator. Not prey. Witness.

Level 4 — The Hospital Corridor
Setting: A long hospital hallway at 3 AM. Motion-activated lights. The shadow must move only when sensors aren't tripped, and a janitor pushes a cart down the corridor.
Mechanic introduced: Triggered light. The shadow's own movement can activate sensors if it crosses certain thresholds.
Design: The hallway has three sections, each with different sensor patterns. The janitor moves slowly but steadily. The shadow has to stay ahead of him without triggering motion lights — which means stillness in some sections, speed in others. The rhythm is deliberately uncomfortable.
Halfway through, an open patient room: A monitor beeps quietly. An old man sleeps under a single dim lamp. The shadow can pause here. Nothing happens. Just the beep, the breath, the dim light that the shadow can almost touch.
Shadow's monologue:

They build their endings under bright lights. They are afraid of what comes for everyone.

Why it doesn't get old: The motion-light mechanic is the first real puzzle. Movement becomes a resource you ration. The hospital setting gives weight to a mechanic that could otherwise feel arbitrary.

Level 5 — The Lighthouse
Setting: The exterior of a working lighthouse on a cliff. The beam rotates on a fixed cycle. The shadow must climb the rocky path to the door at the base.
Mechanic introduced: Predictable rotating light. Long sweep cycles where the shadow has to commit to movement during darkness windows, knowing the light will return.
Design: This is the first level with a clear visual spectacle. The lighthouse beam sweeps across the ocean and the cliffs in long, slow arcs. The shadow's movement becomes choreographed — bursts of motion timed to the rhythm of the light. No enemies. The lighthouse itself is the antagonist.
At the door, the shadow stops. There is no way in. The level ends.
Shadow's monologue:

I have been here before. Different stone. Same purpose. They will build a taller one when this falls.

Why it doesn't get old: The visual scale shifts dramatically — outdoor, vertical, atmospheric. After four interior levels, the openness lands. The unreachable door also seeds a thematic note: the shadow is not trying to win. It is moving through.

Level 6 — The Subway
Setting: An empty late-night subway platform. Train arrivals flood the platform with light every ninety seconds. Between trains, the platform is dim but not dark — emergency lights stay on.
Mechanic introduced: Combined timing and positioning. The shadow has to navigate ambient dim light continuously while preparing for periodic floods of full brightness from arriving trains.
Design: The platform is long. There are pillars, benches, a closed kiosk. The shadow's task is to reach the far tunnel before three trains pass. Each train forces a complete repositioning. Between trains, a homeless man sleeps under a bench. The shadow passes near him repeatedly. He does not stir.
Overheard, a recorded announcement, looping:

"For your safety, please remain behind the yellow line. For your safety, the platform is monitored at all times. For your safety —"

Shadow's monologue (after the third train):

Safety. They have a word for the absence of dark. They do not have a word for the absence of light.

Why it doesn't get old: Two layered timing systems — continuous dim-light navigation and periodic light floods — create the most mechanically demanding puzzle so far. The looping announcement gives the level a hypnotic quality.

Level 7 — The Power Station
Setting: A regional electrical substation. Floodlights everywhere. Cooling fans. The shadow has to cross a maintenance yard to reach a control building.
Mechanic introduced: Interactive shadows. The shadow can briefly pull the shadows of objects — a parked truck, a transformer housing, a stack of crates — to extend its reach. Each pull costs something. The pulled shadow snaps back after a few seconds.
Design: The hardest puzzle in the proof of concept. The yard has no natural path of darkness across it. The player has to chain shadow-pulls in sequence, timing each one so the next is reachable before the previous snaps back. Two security guards patrol with strong flashlights.
Climax of the level: The shadow reaches the control building's exterior. Through a window, it sees a single technician at a console. The technician is alone, monitoring screens. He looks tired.
Shadow's monologue:

This is where they think the light comes from. They are wrong. The light comes from fear. This building only carries it.

Why it doesn't get old: The shadow-pulling mechanic transforms the player from a passive avoider into an active manipulator of the environment. The level peaks the mechanical complexity right before the final level pulls everything back to silence.

Level 8 — The House
Setting: A small house on the edge of a town. Late evening. One lamp on in the living room. A man — middle-aged, alone — is reading.
Mechanic introduced: None. Every mechanic from the previous levels is available, but the level uses almost none of them.
Design: The shadow enters through the gap under the front door. The house is mostly dark — the shadow can move freely. The man reads. He does not look up.
The shadow moves through the rooms. There are photographs. A woman, no longer present. A child, grown. A life lived under warm electric light. The shadow lingers in each room. There is no time pressure.
Eventually, the shadow returns to the living room. It positions itself across from the man, in the dark corner of the room.
The man looks up.
He sees it.
He does not flinch. He does not run. He has been waiting, in a way he could not have explained to anyone.
Dialogue — the only real dialogue in the game:

Man: "You've been here before. Haven't you."
Shadow: Yes.
Man: "How long."
Shadow: Longer than this house. Longer than the word for house.
Man: "And if I turn it off."
Shadow: You know what happens.
Man: "Yeah."
(A long pause.)
Man: "Okay."

He reaches over and clicks off the lamp.
The room goes black. The shadow expands across the entire screen.
There is a long silence. Then — gradually — the shadow contracts back to a small shape. A human shape, briefly. Then less than that. Then nothing where the man was sitting.
The shadow moves to the door. Pauses. Leaves.
Final on-screen text, after the game ends:

The dark is older than this. It will be older than what comes next.

Credits roll over a black screen with no music.
